Practical compliance checks for project owners and facility operators

Environmental compliance is easier to manage when it is planned early, documented clearly, and treated as an ongoing operating responsibility.

Environmental compliance is not only about securing a permit. For many companies, the more difficult work begins after the project is approved, when conditions must be monitored, reports must be submitted, records must be maintained, and operating teams must keep up with changes in site activity.

This guide highlights common mistakes that NBCS often sees in environmental and regulatory support work. It is intended for general information only and should not be treated as legal advice. Requirements can vary depending on project type, location, scale, equipment, waste streams, and applicable agency guidance. When in doubt, project owners should confirm requirements with the appropriate Environmental Management Bureau office or seek professional assistance.

1. Starting or expanding operations without checking ECC or CNC coverage

One of the most common mistakes is assuming that a project does not need environmental screening because it is small, existing, or similar to a previous activity. In practice, the need for an Environmental Compliance Certificate (ECC), Certificate of Non-Coverage (CNC), or further assessment depends on the project category, location, potential impacts, and applicable screening rules.

This problem often appears when a facility adds new equipment, expands production, changes site use, or starts construction before confirming environmental coverage. Even if the original business had previous documentation, the new activity may still need to be reviewed.

How to fix it

  • Screen the project before construction, expansion, or operational changes begin.
  • Keep copies of previous ECC, CNC, permits, site plans, and project descriptions in one compliance folder.
  • Check whether the proposed activity changes the scale, process, discharge, emissions, or waste profile of the site.
  • When uncertain, document the question early and seek guidance before committing to implementation.

2. Treating the ECC as the end of compliance

An ECC is not simply a document to be filed away. It usually contains conditions, commitments, and monitoring requirements that must be implemented during project construction, operation, or both. Companies can run into problems when the person who handled permitting is not the same person managing the site, or when conditions are not translated into practical tasks for operations.

Common examples include missed environmental management plan commitments, incomplete monitoring records, unassigned responsibilities, or conditions that are only rediscovered during an audit.

How to fix it

  • Convert ECC conditions into a simple compliance tracker with responsible persons, due dates, evidence required, and status notes.
  • Brief site managers and operational staff on conditions that affect daily work.
  • Review compliance commitments before major project milestones, inspections, renewals, or audits.
  • Keep proof of implementation, including photos, sampling results, receipts, training records, and correspondence.

3. Missing Compliance Monitoring Report deadlines or submitting incomplete reports

Compliance Monitoring Reports (CMRs) are often treated as routine paperwork, but they are important evidence of how a project is meeting its environmental commitments. Incomplete reports, missing laboratory attachments, vague status updates, or late submissions can create avoidable risk during review.

The issue is usually not lack of intent. It is usually lack of a reporting calendar, scattered supporting documents, or unclear coordination between operations, consultants, laboratories, and management.

How to fix it

  • Maintain a compliance calendar for CMRs, Self-Monitoring Reports, permit renewals, sampling schedules, and other recurring requirements.
  • Start collecting attachments before the reporting deadline, not during the final week.
  • Use consistent language when reporting the status of ECC conditions and environmental management plan commitments.
  • Check that laboratory results, photos, receipts, and monitoring data match the reporting period.

4. Weak hazardous waste documentation and storage control

Hazardous waste compliance can be overlooked when waste volumes are small or generated only occasionally. However, small quantities can still create documentation and handling issues if containers are not labeled, storage areas are not controlled, manifests are incomplete, or records are not kept consistently.

Many problems begin with poor waste identification. If teams do not know which waste streams are hazardous, they may store, transport, or dispose of them incorrectly. This can affect worker safety, environmental risk, and regulatory compliance.

How to fix it

  • Identify and list all waste streams generated by the facility, including occasional maintenance waste.
  • Confirm whether the company needs hazardous waste generator registration or updates to existing records.
  • Use proper labels, containers, segregation, and temporary storage controls.
  • Keep manifests, hauling records, treatment/disposal certificates, and related documents organized by date and waste type.
  • Train staff who handle, store, or coordinate the disposal of regulated waste.

5. Forgetting that air emission sources may need separate permits

Generators, boilers, ovens, process equipment, and other air pollution source installations may require separate review or permitting depending on the equipment, emissions, capacity, and applicable rules. These requirements are sometimes missed because the equipment is viewed as auxiliary, temporary, or already part of the facility.

The risk increases when equipment is purchased, relocated, or operated before the compliance team reviews the permitting implications. In some cases, technical documents such as engineer reports, layouts, specifications, or source information may be needed.

How to fix it

  • Create an inventory of equipment that may generate emissions, including standby units.
  • Review air permitting requirements before buying, installing, or modifying equipment.
  • Keep specifications, nameplate photos, layout drawings, and process information available for technical review.
  • Track permit validity, renewal periods, emission testing schedules, and related compliance evidence.

A better approach: build a simple compliance system

Most compliance problems are preventable. The solution is not always a complicated system. For many facilities, the biggest improvement is to create a practical compliance file, assign clear responsibilities, and review requirements before changes happen.

A simple environmental compliance system should include:

  • a master list of permits, certificates, registrations, and conditions;
  • a calendar of recurring submissions, renewals, and monitoring activities;
  • a folder for laboratory results, manifests, photos, receipts, reports, and correspondence;
  • a process for reviewing environmental requirements before project changes; and
  • regular internal checks so issues are found before audits or deadlines.

Environmental compliance is easier when it is part of project planning and daily operations. By identifying obligations early, keeping records organized, and reviewing requirements before changes are made, companies can reduce delays, avoid repeated findings, and make better decisions for both operations and the environment.
